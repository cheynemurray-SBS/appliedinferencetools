Tool 2 · Sampling Distribution & CLT

IRMC scenario — step 2 of 4

Individual patient readmission wait times are right-skewed — a few patients take much longer. Why can we still use normal-distribution methods on our sample mean of 16.8 days? The Central Limit Theorem explains it. Explore how n transforms the sampling distribution.

The Central Limit Theorem

Regardless of the population's shape, the sampling distribution of x̄ approaches normal as n grows. Its mean equals μ, and its standard deviation — the standard error — equals σ/√n. With n = 45 and σ ≈ 4.2, the standard error is already small enough to trust normal-based inference.

Why this matters for IRMC

Even though individual readmission wait times are skewed (a small number of complex cases can take 40+ days), the mean across 45 patients follows an approximately normal distribution with SE = 4.2/√45 ≈ 0.63 days. This is the distribution we reason about when building confidence intervals and running t-tests.

CLT rule of thumb for business analysts

  1. n ≥ 30: CLT applies for most business distributions (moderately skewed data)
  2. n ≥ 50: Reliable even for heavily skewed distributions (e.g. revenue, wait times)
  3. n < 30: Normality of the underlying population must be verified or assumed

⚠ Correct language for any executive presentation

A 95% CI does not mean there is a 95% probability the true mean is in this specific interval. The interval either contains μ or it doesn't. What 95% describes is the long-run reliability of the procedure. Say: "We are 95% confident this interval captures the true mean."

Tool 4 · One-Sample t-test

IRMC scenario — step 4 of 4

The CI suggests wait times may have improved, but leadership wants a formal test. H₀: the care-coordination program has not changed mean readmission wait time (μ = 18.4 days). H₁: mean wait time has decreased (μ < 18.4 days). Use the sample data to evaluate the evidence — one-tailed at α = 0.05.

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)     df = n − 1

Statistical power at this sample size

With n = 45, σ ≈ 4.2, and a true effect of ~1.6 days (as observed), the approximate power of this one-tailed test at α = 0.05 is ~85% — meaning there was an 85% probability of detecting a difference this large if it truly exists. Adequate for a clinical quality-improvement study.

Executive Memo — Draft language

"Analysis of 45 randomly selected patient records indicates that mean 30-day readmission wait time has decreased to 16.8 days (95% CI: 15.6–18.0 days), compared to the historical benchmark of 18.4 days. A one-sample t-test confirms this reduction is statistically significant (t = −2.75, df = 44, p = 0.004) and represents a clinically meaningful effect (d = 0.41). The data support continued investment in the care-coordination program. Recommended next step: a controlled before-after design to attribute causation."

What the data cannot tell us

This is an observational sample, not a controlled experiment. The improvement could reflect the program, changes in patient mix, seasonal effects, or regression to the mean. Statistical inference establishes what is likely to be true about the population; it does not establish causation. The next analytical step is a controlled study or interrupted time-series design.
